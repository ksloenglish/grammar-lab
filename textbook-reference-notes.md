## Visible teaching points

Comparatives compare two people, places, things or actions. Than often follows the comparative; it may be omitted when the other item is already understood. Short words normally add -er; e-ending words add -r; consonant-y examples use -y + -ier; suitable short consonants double (big/bigger, fat/fatter). Good/well become better; bad/badly become worse; far has farther/further. Longer adjectives/adverbs usually use more or less.

Some two-syllable adjectives allow both patterns: cleverer/more clever, politer/more polite, commoner/more common, quieter/more quiet. The textbook also shows louder/more loudly and slower/more slowly as alternative adjective/adverb word-family patterns; do NOT teach adding -er to loudly/slowly themselves. Strengthen comparisons with much, far, a lot; soften with a bit, a little, slightly.

Superlatives compare one item with a group of three or more. Usually use the; short forms -est/-st, y changes -y + -iest, doubling big/biggest and fat/fattest. Good/well → best; bad/badly → worst; far → farthest/furthest. Longer forms use the most/the least. Two forms may work: cleverest/the most clever and quietest/the most quiet; adverbs loudest/most loudly and slowest/most slowly. Superlative adjective patterns include one of + plural noun; of/among + plural group; in + place/group; has/have ever + past participle. Example meaning: one of the busiest cities; the funniest among friends; the hardest in a class; the best film I have ever seen.

As ... as uses the base adjective/adverb for equality; not as ... as indicates a lower level and corresponds to less ... than. Correct negative adverb order: Your dog does not bark as loudly as Terry's dog; not Your dog barks not as loudly. Avoid combining more/most with -er/-est (more fatter, most easiest, most earliest). Compare the intended matching items: exam results better than mine, hair as long as my sister's. For actions, the textbook accepts earlier than I did / me.
